ARTICLE 1

A stop closes a hypothesis

Persistence in a method can mean closing a trade when its predefined limit is reached. The stop does not answer who you are; it tests whether the hypothesis still deserves capital under the conditions you accepted.

ARTICLE 2

Why losses hurt

Loss aversion and reference points can make an unrealized loss feel repairable. The disposition effect describes the tendency to realize gains early and hold losses too long. Avoiding the discomfort of realization can reinforce risk expansion.

ARTICLE 3

Automatic thoughts

“It will come back” or “I cannot finish negative” may function as justifications for changing a rule. The goal is not positive thinking; it is to test whether the interpretation fits the plan and available evidence.

ARTICLE 4

Accept discomfort without obeying it

Acceptance and cognitive defusion create space between a thought and an action. You do not need to eliminate anxiety or frustration before following the plan; avoid giving those experiences control of the decision.

ARTICLE 5

Precommitment

The moment of maximum emotional pressure is a poor time to redesign risk rules. Define invalidation, size, maximum loss, and permitted adjustments before entry.

ARTICLE 6

If–then plans

Turn intentions into observable actions: “If I want to widen the stop, then I will pause and check the written rule before touching the order.” Make the trigger concrete and the response simple.

ARTICLE 7

After the stop

The next trade has no debt to the previous one. Pause, record the trigger, check whether a new setup exists independently, and follow predefined session limits.

ARTICLE 8

Gradual practice

Use historical review, replay, and fictional scenarios to rehearse the response. Do not create real losses just to practice tolerating a stop.

ARTICLE 9

Process metrics

A losing trade can be well executed; a profitable trade can violate the plan. Score whether setup, risk, size, and stop rules were followed—not only P&L.

ARTICLE 10

Shame and identity

“I made a mistake” describes behavior; “I am a failure” condemns identity. Replace global judgment with a specific correction and a concrete next action.

ARTICLE 11

Counterfactual regret

Price may reverse after the stop. Review what was known before the decision, not what became obvious afterward. Future information is not evidence that the original rule was wrong.

ARTICLE 12

Sunk costs

Money, time, and effort already invested do not make a position more valid. Ask whether you would open the same position now, at this price and risk, if you were flat.

ARTICLE 13

The need to be right

Perfectionism can make a stop feel like a verdict. In a probabilistic environment, a reasonable decision can have a negative result; assess process quality separately.

ARTICLE 14

Uncertainty

A stop does not remove uncertainty; it limits consequences. Separate what was controllable from what was not, and do not treat discomfort as proof that the rule is wrong.

ARTICLE 15

Post-stop FOMO

After exiting, fear may shift from losing money to missing a move. A new entry must meet the method's criteria rather than compensate for the previous exit.

ARTICLE 16

Disciplined self-compassion

Speak to yourself as you would to a competent colleague who made the same mistake. Then name one specific repair; compassion is not an excuse to ignore the rule.

ARTICLE 17

The recovery trap

“Back to breakeven” can turn a balance into an emotional target. The market does not owe a recovery, and a new trade should not be assigned the job of erasing the last result.

ARTICLE 18

Emotional spillover

Sleep, conflict, urgency, and other events can enter the trading session. A brief check-in before the first decision helps distinguish market evidence from unrelated emotional load.

ARTICLE 19

“Tomorrow I’ll do it right”

A promise about the future does not repair a present violation. Define the smallest observable behavior to practice next session and arrange the environment to support it.

ARTICLE 20

Early profits and overconfidence

A quick win is not proof of robust skill. Distinguish a favorable outcome from a repeatable process and avoid escalating size or frequency because recent trades felt easy.

ARTICLE 21

P&L as an emotional scoreboard

Financial result, process quality, and emotional state are different variables. A green day can include serious rule violations; a red day can include sound execution.

ARTICLE 22

Values versus goals

A goal is an outcome; a value is a direction for behavior. Choose the professional qualities you want to express under uncertainty, then translate them into observable rules.

ARTICLE 23

Responsibility without shame

Responsibility means identifying what was under your control and repairing it. Shame can obscure the specific behavior that needs to change.

ARTICLE 24

Anatomy of a tilt day

A difficult session often starts before the largest loss. Track the first trigger, the first changed thought, the first rule violation, and the point where the chain could have been interrupted.

ARTICLE 25

Self-talk becomes action

An automatic phrase can lead to emotion, impulse, action, and consequence. Write the chain down and design an alternative response at the earliest realistic point.

ARTICLE 26

Short winners, long losers

The disposition effect is a research finding, not a diagnosis of every trader. Compare time in winning and losing positions with your strategy's actual rules and expected behavior.

ARTICLE 27

Manage, don’t suppress

Emotion regulation does not require becoming a robot. Notice the emotion, name the urge, return to the written rule, and choose an action aligned with the plan.

ARTICLE 28

Self-justifying biases

The mind can manufacture explanations for a preferred action. Seek disconfirming evidence and separate a new market fact from a story designed to defend the position.

ARTICLE 29

Context reset

Rebuild the current thesis: what changed, what supports it, what contradicts it, and what would you write if you were flat? A reset updates the view instead of defending the past.

ARTICLE 30

Corrective replay

Revisit a critical moment using only information available at that time. Pause, debrief, return to the scene, and rehearse the response that should have followed the rule.

ARTICLE 31

Here and now

Separate observable fact, story, thought, rule, and action. A reversal may be possible; the error is using an imagined future or hypothetical profit to justify breaking the current rule.
