How to read the Risk/Reward × Win Rate table

Why low win rates can still be profitable, why high win rates can still lose money, and why none of this matters if the account runs out of margin first.

What the table shows

The table combines two characteristics of a trading strategy: the risk/reward ratio (how much you risk per trade compared with how much you aim to make) and the win rate (the percentage of trades that reach the target). Each cell answers a simple question: if I repeated this pattern many times, would I tend to make money, lose money, or break even over the long run?

Red means negative expectancy, orange means breakeven, and green means positive expectancy. The boundary between red and green is diagonal: the less you aim to gain relative to what you risk, the higher your win rate must be. The greater your reward relative to risk, the lower the required win rate.

The math behind the colors

Let R be the amount at risk (the stop loss, in currency), G the amount gained at the target, and W the win rate between 0 and 1. The average result per trade, or mathematical expectancy, is:

E = W × G − (1 − W) × R

A cell is orange when E = 0. Solving for W gives the breakeven win rate:

Wbreakeven = R ÷ (R + G)

Examples that match the table: with risk 1 and reward 1 (1:1), breakeven is 50%. At 1:2 it is 33.3%; at 1:4, 20%. In the opposite direction, at 4:1 (risk 4 to make 1), you need an 80% win rate just to break even. At 9:1, you need 90%.

Reading a few cells

  • A 70% win rate with a 3:1 risk/reward ratio loses money. Out of 10 trades, 7 gain 1 and 3 lose 3. The net is 7 − 9 = −2. Traders who “are almost always right” can still lose money when losses are allowed to grow.
  • A 30% win rate with 1:3 risk/reward can be profitable. Out of 10 trades, 3 gain 3 and 7 lose 1. The net is 9 − 7 = +2. Losing seven trades out of ten and still making money is entirely possible.
  • A 50% win rate needs slightly more than 1:1 after costs. At 1:1 and 50%, the table shows exact breakeven before costs. Trading costs can turn that result negative.

First caution: the two variables are not independent

The table treats win rate and risk/reward ratio as separate controls, as if one could change without affecting the other. Real trading is not like that. If you increase the target while keeping the stop fixed, price has farther to travel in your direction before reaching the target, so the win rate may fall. If you shorten the target, the win rate may rise, but reward per trade declines. The table shows what is mathematically required to profit; it does not show what your method can actually achieve. Only your trade history (backtest or live trades) can answer that, and the win rate should be measured for the exact stop/target pair you use.

Second caution: costs shift breakeven

Commissions, exchange fees, and slippage (the difference between the intended and actual execution price) increase losses and reduce gains. With a per-contract cost of c, the formula becomes:

Wbreakeven = (R + c) ÷ (R + G)

Example for WINFUT: 1 contract, a 100-point stop (R$ 20) and a 200-point target (R$ 40). With no costs, breakeven is 20 ÷ 60 = 33.3%. With R$ 2 in round-trip costs, the actual loss becomes R$ 22, the gain R$ 38, and breakeven rises to 22 ÷ 60 = 36.7%. The smaller the stop, the larger the relative effect of costs—so very short trades deserve particular attention to this number. The simulator includes a cost field and recolors the table accordingly.

Third caution: positive expectancy does not mean survival

This is the most important point missing from the original table. Expectancy describes the average across a large number of trades, but you experience one particular sequence, with its own variance. A strategy with a 40% win rate loses 6 out of every 10 trades, including losing streaks. One useful rough estimate for the longest expected losing streak in N trades is:

streak ≈ ln(N) ÷ ln(1 ÷ (1 − W))

At W = 40% and N = 200 trades, this estimate is about 10 consecutive losses. A worse-than-average sequence can be longer. Ten consecutive R$ 20 stops consume R$ 200, before any recovery. Two green cells with the same expectancy can produce very different equity curves: one smooth, the other with sharp declines. That is why the simulator does not rely on the average alone; it draws random sequences of wins and losses.

Capital, margin, and risk per trade

Futures trading requires two buffers. The first is the required margin for holding positions: if capital falls below it, you cannot trade the planned number of contracts. The second is a drawdown buffer: enough capital to survive an adverse sequence without reaching the margin threshold. The simulator estimates suggested capital as:

suggested capital = total margin + 95th-percentile maximum drawdown

The 95th percentile means that in 95% of simulated sequences, maximum drawdown was at or below that value. It is a reference, not a guarantee. A common risk-management guideline is to risk 1%–2% of capital per trade; the simulator shows how much your stop represents relative to capital. If one stop consumes 10% of the account, only a few consecutive losses may be enough to end trading.

Warning: if expectancy is negative (a red cell), increasing capital will not fix the strategy. More capital only delays the outcome. The ruin warning can appear even when the account balance looks large.

How to use the tool

  • Choose the instrument, contract count, stop and target in points, capital to keep in the account, and cost per contract.
  • The blue row is your exact scenario. The other rows show how the same account would behave with different risk/reward ratios.
  • Click a cell to see the equity curve: blue is one random sequence, dashed green is the expected average, and dashed red is the margin threshold.
  • Choose “Ruin probability” under Cell labels to see which table cells have a risk of falling below margin at the capital entered.
  • If a red warning appears, reduce the contract count, reduce the stop, or increase capital, and observe how the probability changes.

Model limitations

  • Independent trades. Assumes each trade is independent and that every trade has the same stop, target, and win rate. Real markets have favorable and unfavorable periods for a given method.
  • Simplified execution. Does not include gaps, partial exits, slippage beyond the average entered, daily loss limits, or reinvestment of profits (compounding).
  • Uncertain win rate. A win rate measured from few trades is uncertain. With 30 trades, an observed 50% win rate could reflect an underlying rate closer to 35% or 65% by chance.
  • Variable margin. Entered margin is a reference and changes by broker, instrument, and time of day (day-trading vs. overnight margin).
